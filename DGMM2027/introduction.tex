\section{Introduction}

\noindent\textbf{Context.} When analyzing discrete data like point clouds, determining the
local intrinsic dimension of the data is crucial for its
further geometric or semantic analysis. The same holds when analyzing
digital sets. For instance, in 3D biomedical imaging, analyzing
geometrically vascular or airway networks must be carried out
differently than thin bones or organs. In material engineering,
polymeric or metal foams contain 1D filaments or 2D surfaces depending
on their manufacturing. Their topology and geometry strongly influence
macroscopic physical characteristics, like anisotropic rigidity or
elasticity.

\noindent\textbf{Contribution.}  We introduce a family of geometric
invariants based on the volumes of coordinate projections for the
local analysis of digital sets. For a \(k\)-dimensional Euclidean ball
of radius \(r\), we show that the sum of the squared \(m\)-dimensional
projection volumes equals \(\omega_m^2\binom{k}{m}r^{2m}\), whatever
the orientation of the ball or the ambient dimension. These identities
give geometric characterizations of intrinsic dimension through
coordinate projections. We then establish stability estimates for
local neighborhoods of smooth submanifolds and show how these
estimates extend to their standard cubical discretizations. They lead
to local intrinsic dimension estimators with explicit approximation
bounds. We further show that the individual projection volumes provide
estimates of the absolute components of the local unit normal vector
on digitized surfaces.

\noindent\textbf{Related works.}  For point clouds, the main
approaches to estimating local intrinsic dimension include analyzing
the spectrum of local covariance matrices \cite{Fukunaga:1971},
studying the scaling of neighborhood point counts or nearest-neighbor
distances \cite{Levina:2004,Farahmand:2007}, and constructing
multiscale affine approximations \cite{Allard:2012}.  Nonlinear
dimensionality reduction provides another perspective, notably through
Isomap \cite{Tenenbaum:2000}, which approximates geodesic distances
using nearest-neighbor graphs, and locally linear embedding
\cite{Roweis:2000}, which preserves local affine reconstruction
weights in a low-dimensional embedding.  For digital sets, related
geometric approaches often rely on local approximation by digital
lines, planes, or hyperplanes (e.g. see
\cite{Klette:2004}). Extensions and algorithms in higher dimensions
have also been investigated
\cite{Gerard:2005,Charrier:2011,Provot:2011}.  In contrast, our
approach relies on geometric invariants constructed from coordinate
projection volumes. These invariants admit exact
orientation-independent identities and quantitative stability bounds,
while being easy to compute on cubical representations.

\noindent\textbf{Organisation.}  In Section~\ref{sec-invariants}, we
define our geometric invariants, which are related to the Pythagorean
formula for volumes
\cite{Conant01031974,Czyzewska:1991,ancel2023pythagorean}. Applied to
a ball, these invariants depend only on its affine dimension. We
show their stability with respect to Hausdorff perturbation, as
well as to their discretization by covering of cubes, enabling
intrinsic dimension analysis of digital
sets. Section~\ref{sec-normals} highlights the link between projection
volumes and the absolute components of the local normal
vector. Section~\ref{sec-experiments} provides an experimental
illustration of our method, and concludes with a few perspectives.
